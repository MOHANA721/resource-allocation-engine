1. Summary of findings

  • All three algorithms share one rule set and one cost function. Differences in results therefore come only from how each algorithm searches, which makes the comparison fair.
  • Hungarian achieves the lowest total cost in the default job-count mode. This is verified against an exhaustive brute-force search on 30 small scenarios at three weight settings, and against the other two algorithms on 60 larger ones. With priority-5 protection on, it first serves as many priority-5 requests as possible, then minimizes cost.
  • Greedy is on average 2.6% to 15.9% above optimal on random scenarios, depending on demand and skill scarcity. Its loss is largest when technicians have several skills and demand is moderate to heavy, because it spends flexible technicians early.
  • Regret is on average 0.8% to 14.8% above optimal and beats greedy on average at every demand level tested. It handles the highest priority first, and within a priority serves first the request with most to lose. An earlier version ranked by regret alone and was 25.4% above optimal under overload; Section 8.4 shows the case and the fix.
  • Shorter urgent travel is not a better plan. Greedy consistently sends technicians a shorter distance to urgent jobs, yet loses on total cost, because those short trips take technicians that other jobs needed.
  • The weights define what "best" means. If leaving a job open is priced too cheaply, the optimal plan drops urgent customers. The Always serve P5 setting, on by default, prevents that for priority-5 requests (Section 8.5). Other weights should still be set from business policy before the algorithms are compared.
  • Capacity can be measured in working minutes as well as job count. All three algorithms then fit the travel and work of each technician's jobs inside his shift length (Section 8.9).

2. Problem and scope

A service company has a fixed team of technicians, each based in a Bengaluru locality, each with one or two trade skills, a shift, and a maximum number of jobs per day. Households submit repair requests, each requiring one skill within a customer time window, taking a known duration, and carrying a priority from 1 (can wait) to 5 (urgent).

The engine decides which technician serves which request, and which requests remain unserved when capacity is insufficient. It solves the problem with three algorithms, reports the same metrics for each, and visualizes the three plans side by side on a map.

In scope: skills, capacity, time windows, shifts, travel time, priority, workload balance, explanations, metrics, and comparison. Out of scope by design: ordering a technician's jobs into a route (vehicle routing), road networks, and requests that arrive during the day. These are discussed in Section 16.

3.3 Assignment and result

For each request, each algorithm returns an assignment containing the technician (or none), the distance, the cost charged, and a plain-language explanation. Each algorithm also returns a set of metrics (Section 10). Requests whose skill no technician has are returned separately as skill gaps.

4.2 Design decisions

  • Reproducibility. All randomness uses random.Random(seed); identical settings always produce an identical scenario.
  • Fixed workforce. Technicians are generated first and never altered to fit demand, as in a real company.
  • Honest metrics. A request needing a skill nobody has is reported as a skill gap and excluded from every algorithm's score, so "unserved" always reflects an algorithm's decision rather than impossible data.
  • Fair comparison. One API call runs all three algorithms on the identical input with the identical rules and cost.

5. Hard constraints

A technician may serve a request only if all four conditions hold, plus a fifth in working-minutes mode. A pair that fails any condition is never considered by any algorithm.

6. Soft constraints and the cost function

Among feasible options, the algorithms prefer lower cost. Three soft constraints are combined into one number:

assignment cost   = w_distance × km  +  w_balance × (jobs the technician already holds)
unserved penalty  = w_unassigned × priority
total cost        = Σ assignment costs  +  Σ unserved penalties

Defaults: w_distance = 1, w_balance = 2, w_unassigned = 30.

Situation (defaults)CalculationCost
Technician with no jobs yet, 7.5 km away1 × 7.5 + 2 × 07.5
Same technician, second job1 × 7.5 + 2 × 19.5
Same technician, third job1 × 7.5 + 2 × 211.5
Priority-5 request left unserved30 × 5150
Priority-1 request left unserved30 × 130
  • Distance represents travel time and fuel.
  • Balance discourages the nearest technician from absorbing every nearby job while others are idle.
  • Unserved penalty gives a price to disappointing a customer, scaled by urgency. Without it, serving nobody would cost zero.

6.1 When a request is left open on purpose

All three algorithms leave a servable request open when serving it would cost more than its unserved penalty: greedy and regret compare the request's cheapest option with its penalty, and Hungarian compares whole plans. At the default weights this does not occur in practice (no such case in 30 scenarios of 32 requests); it matters when the unassigned weight is low.

Always serve P5 (must_serve_p5, on by default) is a policy rule on top of the weights: a priority-5 request is never left open on purpose. A priority-5 request is then unserved only if no technician can take it, or if every way of serving it leaves another priority-5 request open. The reported cost of an unserved request is still w_unassigned × priority, so totals remain comparable.

7. Algorithms

7.1 Greedy (baseline)

Method. Sort requests by priority (highest first), then earliest window close, then id. For each request in turn, assign the cheapest feasible technician with capacity, unless that costs more than the request's unserved penalty (Section 6.1). Decisions are never revisited.

Complexity. About O(requests × technicians); about 5–8 ms at the largest setting.

Explanation given. "Cheapest of N feasible technicians; runner-up X."

7.2 Hungarian (optimal reference)

Method. Build a cost matrix of requests × technician slots plus one "unserved" column per request, and solve it with scipy.optimize.linear_sum_assignment.

Complexity. O(n³) on the matrix size; 1–3 ms for 10 technicians and about 20–40 ms at the largest setting.

Explanation given. "Chosen in the minimum-cost matching; X is closer and is kept for another request."

7.3 Regret (custom heuristic)

Method. For each open request, regret = cost of second-best option − cost of best; a request with one option has infinite regret. Among the open requests with the highest priority, serve the one with the largest regret, then repeat. Ties go to the earlier window close. Requests are left open on the same terms as greedy (Section 6.1).

Complexity. Each request's options are built once. After each assignment, only the chosen technician's entry is updated in the requests that list him, so distances are never recomputed. About 12–16 ms at the largest setting (300–400 ms before this optimization).

Explanation given. "Chosen early: among the highest-priority requests left, it had the largest regret."

7.4 How Hungarian handles multi-job capacity and unserved requests

The Hungarian method assigns one row to one column. These extensions adapt it to this problem:

  • Slots. A technician with max_jobs = 3 becomes three columns. Slot k costs distance + w_balance × k, so the solver fills a technician's cheaper slots first and the total balance charge equals that of the other algorithms.
  • Unserved columns. Each request has its own column priced at w_unassigned × priority, allowing the solver to leave a request unserved when serving it would cost more.
  • Priority-5 guard. With Always serve P5 on, a priority-5 request's unserved column also carries a guard larger than the cost of any complete plan. The solver therefore serves as many priority-5 requests as possible before it considers cost. The guard is not included in reported costs.
  • Infeasible cells (wrong skill, late arrival, shift overrun, or another request's unserved column) receive a blocking cost larger than leaving every request unserved, so they are never chosen.
  • Working minutes. An assignment matrix cannot add up time across a technician's jobs. In minutes mode, if the solution puts a technician past his working time, his longest job is forbidden for him and the matrix is solved again, until no technician is over. The plan is always feasible but, in this mode only, not guaranteed optimal: greedy or regret was cheaper in 4 of 120 comparisons (Section 11.4).

8. Case studies

Each case is small enough to verify by hand. All figures are produced by the engine and most are asserted in the test suite.

8.1 Greedy strands a specialist (the "trap" scenario)

SetupVikram (Koramangala) has electrical and plumbing; Arjun (Jayanagar) has plumbing only; each can take one job. A plumbing leak near Vikram closes at 12:00; an electrical panel that only Vikram can serve closes at 16:00. Both are priority 5. Default weights.

GreedyBoth are priority 5; the leak closes first, so it is processed first and takes its cheapest option, Vikram (0.26). The panel then has no feasible technician and is left unserved (150).

RegretBoth requests are priority 5, so regret decides. Leak regret = 4.63 − 0.26 = 4.37; panel has one option, so regret is infinite. The panel is served first by Vikram (1.65), then the leak by Arjun (4.63).

HungarianSelects the cheaper whole plan directly from the matrix in Section 7.4.

GreedyVikramunserved150.261143.99

RegretArjunVikram6.2700

LessonGreedy used the flexible technician on the job that had a backup. Greedy's urgent travel (0.26 km) is far shorter than the others' (6.27 km), but only because an urgent job was never visited.

8.2 Greedy loses a small amount through order alone

SetupRahul and Karan, both plumbers with capacity 2, about 1 km apart. Two equal-priority jobs, r1 and r2, both about 0.11 km from Rahul. Karan is 0.98 km from r1 and 1.09 km from r2. Default weights.

GreedyTakes r1 first: Rahul (0.11). For r2, Rahul now costs 0.11 + 2 = 2.11, so Karan (1.09) is chosen. Total 1.20.

Hungarian and RegretSend Karan to r1 (0.98) and Rahul to r2 (0.11). Total 1.09.

LessonEven with no scarcity, processing order matters: the job served second absorbed the more expensive option. If the balance weight is set to 0, all three let Rahul take both jobs (total 0.22).

8.3 All algorithms tie when resources are plentiful

SetupA plumber next to a plumbing job and an HVAC technician next to an HVAC job, each with spare capacity.

ResultAll three produce the same plan at a total cost of 0.17. When every request has an obvious, uncontested best technician, the extra search in Hungarian and regret brings no benefit.

8.4 Regret under overload: the failure and its fix

SetupVikram (electrical + plumbing) and Arjun (plumbing), one job each. Three requests: an electrical switch (priority 1, only Vikram), a leak (priority 5), and a burst pipe (priority 5); both technicians can serve either plumbing job. One request must be left unserved.

Earlier regretRanked by regret alone. The switch has one option, so its regret is infinite and it took Vikram first. The two priority-5 jobs then shared Arjun, and the burst pipe was left unserved.

Current regretRanks by priority first. The two priority-5 jobs are served before the switch, so the switch is the one left unserved, as with greedy and Hungarian.

9.4 Which algorithm responds to which weight

AlgorithmDistance and balanceUnassigned
GreedyYes: they set which technician is cheapest.Yes: a request is left open when its cheapest option costs more than its penalty.
RegretYes: they set the cheapest technician and the regret used for ordering.Yes, as for greedy.
HungarianYes.Yes: it sets the point at which leaving a request unserved is cheaper than serving it, judged over the whole plan.

Before this version, greedy and regret served every servable request, so the unassigned weight changed only their reported cost. Now all three use it to decide. At the default weights it rarely changes a choice; at low values it does (Section 8.8). These properties are asserted in the test suite. Scaling all three weights by the same factor leaves every plan unchanged and scales every total by that factor.

Recommendation. Set the weights from business policy first (for example, "how many kilometres of travel is one unserved priority-5 customer worth?"), then compare algorithms. All three are always scored with the same weights, so the comparison remains fair at any setting.

12. Conclusions: which algorithm wins, and why

Hungarian wins on total cost in every scenario in job-count mode. It solves the full assignment exactly, so no plan satisfying the same constraints has a lower cost under the same weights; with Always serve P5 on, it is optimal among plans that serve the most priority-5 requests. In working-minutes mode it is near-optimal (cheapest in 116 of 120 comparisons). It remains fast at the sizes supported: 1–3 ms for 10 technicians and about 20–40 ms at the largest setting (40 technicians, 80 requests), against about 5–8 ms for greedy and 12–16 ms for regret.

Recommended use: Hungarian for the planned batch; greedy for decisions that must be made instantly; regret where a simple, explainable heuristic is wanted and Hungarian is not available. Regret beats greedy on average at every demand level tested, but not in every individual scenario.

13. Real-time requests

The application solves a known batch. For requests arriving during the day:

  • Greedy is naturally online: each arriving request can be assigned instantly to the cheapest feasible technician with capacity.
  • Hungarian needs several pending requests to add value; with one request it reduces to greedy. It is used by re-solving periodically over all not-yet-started jobs (a rolling horizon).
  • Regret needs at least two pending requests to compare and is likewise a batch method.

A practical design combines them: greedy gives the customer an immediate confirmation, and Hungarian re-optimizes unstarted jobs every few minutes, with jobs in progress fixed and a small penalty for reassigning a technician to limit disruption.

16. Assumptions, limitations, and proposed solutions

16.1 Assumptions

  • All requests for the planning period are known before planning starts.
  • Technicians start from home at shift start; travel is straight-line at a constant 40 km/h.
  • A technician is equally capable in each of his skills.
  • Data are synthetic but realistic: real Bengaluru localities, mixed shifts, skewed skill demand.
